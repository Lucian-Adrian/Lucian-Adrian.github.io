FlatChat grew through roommate matching, then lost its independence

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 4 min read
FlatChat · 2018Copy link

FlatChat addressed a part of the housing search that property listing sites often left to chance: finding someone compatible to share a home with. Its mobile app helped students and young professionals look for rooms, flatmates and tenants, then start a conversation directly. The product gained a large early user base in India and expanded to Singapore. But after property portal CommonFloor acquired control, and then Quikr acquired CommonFloor, FlatChat’s team and product were drawn into a larger company. Quikr said the app would continue independently for a time, yet it was last updated in 2016 and was removed from Google Play in 2018.

Roommate search was the product, not a side feature

FlatChat grew from Flat.to, a student and bachelor rental site. Its founders later focused on a mobile product centered on flatmate discovery. Instead of browsing only properties, renters could present themselves, review likely roommates and message people who wanted to share or rent a home. The product fit an urban rental need: someone moving to a new city could search for both a room and a person to share it with.

In June 2015, TechCrunch reported that FlatChat had raised $2.5 million from CommonFloor and claimed 50,000 users in its first six months. It also reported 350,000 messages exchanged through the app in May. By August 2016, the Economic Times said FlatChat had around 300,000 users and roughly 52 employees. These are company or publication-reported engagement figures, not evidence of revenue or long-term retention, but they show that the product reached a real audience.

A strategic acquisition changed the conditions

CommonFloor acquired a controlling stake in FlatChat in 2014. In January 2016, Quikr acquired CommonFloor to expand its real estate business. Six months later, Quikr shut FlatChat’s Bengaluru office as it consolidated offices and teams. The Economic Times reported that most of FlatChat’s people left or were offered a transfer, while Quikr absorbed the technology team. Employees said they had received short notice and that the company’s focus was shifting to profitability.

Quikr told the paper that FlatChat’s assets had been merged with Quikr Nxt, its messaging service. Other coverage recorded a more nuanced message: the FlatChat app would remain active as an independent service “for the time being.” MediaNama’s 2016 report captured both accounts, as well as early signs that the app had few active users in some locations and had technical and support problems. This is not a simple story of an acquisition immediately closing the product. Quikr initially said the service would continue, but the standalone operation and team were no longer intact.

The app’s later disappearance is the clearest endpoint

FlatChat’s identity became harder to follow after the integration. Flat.to’s site, the FlatChat product and Quikr’s broader real estate portfolio were related, but not interchangeable. A later historical record from AppBrain says the original FlatChat app was last updated on 5 October 2016 and removed from Google Play on 1 July 2018. The listing is no longer available through Google Play.

The careful conclusion is that FlatChat ceased to operate as a standalone roommate-finding app. The evidence does not establish that every feature or listing was immediately shut off in 2016, nor that Quikr’s property services ended. FlatChat’s product lost its own team and dedicated investment after consolidation, and its Android app later disappeared. The company history reflects both a product integration and an eventual decline in standalone availability.

There were reasonable strategic motives on Quikr’s side. Bringing teams and messaging tools into one platform could reduce duplicated costs and connect property search with chat. But FlatChat served a distinct use case, and its user network depended on a critical mass of renters and room listings in each city. After an acquisition, a parent company must decide whether it will continue to fund and measure that specific job, or absorb the people and technology for another purpose. Without an explicit owner and roadmap, a product can remain nominally alive while its ability to improve fades.

What Domi can take from this

For Domi, FlatChat is a reminder that roommate compatibility and property search should be connected deliberately. If a renter joins a group or creates a profile before inventory is broad, the product can still help people form a household and clarify what they need. That may support distribution among students and early-career renters. Domi should measure completed household matches, inquiries and viewings in a specific location instead of counting profiles alone.

It also suggests protecting the product’s purpose as the team expands. Domi should know which features help renters find a home together and which merely add activity. If roommate discovery brings users in, that should remain a visible product outcome with a responsible owner. A small city or student-community test can reveal whether people come back, invite others and progress to viewings. FlatChat’s reported user numbers were promising, but they did not guarantee the product would retain a dedicated team after control changed.

Quarters closed its U.S. co-living business while its European parent was restructured

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 5 min read
Quarters · 2021Copy link

Quarters began in Berlin as a way for professionals and students to move into furnished shared homes without assembling a household from scratch. Rooms came with furniture and utilities, and a resident could join a managed community rather than negotiate every detail with new housemates. The company later used that model to expand to American cities. In January 2021, its U.S. operation closed and returned leases to landlords. The European business continued through a separate restructuring and sale, so this is a story about a market exit and subsidiary failures, not the disappearance of every Quarters property or the end of co-living as a brand.

From Berlin rooms to a transatlantic operator

The company opened a Berlin co-living space in 2011 and later expanded under the Medici Living name before building the Quarters brand. It targeted people moving for work or study who wanted private rooms, furnished common areas and a simpler path into a new city. The model gave landlords one operating partner and residents one contact for furnished housing and services.

Quarters carried that model to the United States, reaching New York, Brooklyn, Washington, D.C., Chicago and Philadelphia. Its footprint included multiple properties in several cities, and the company presented itself as a major co-living provider on both sides of the Atlantic. Commercial Observer reported that the company had eight U.S. locations and had raised $300 million in 2019 toward a planned expansion of 1,300 units.

There was a sound customer insight behind the expansion. Furnished shared housing makes a move less complicated, especially for a person arriving alone or staying for a limited term. Quarters bundled room, furnishings and some household services, while the company could reuse a recognizable product across several markets. But every new city still required local leases, property relationships, occupancy and service coverage. A common brand did not make those local economics identical.

U.S. expansion carried local lease risk

Quarters’s growth plan left it exposed to buildings and leases that could not be switched off as quickly as demand changed. The pandemic slowed leasing demand and raised questions about the viability of shared living in major cities. When room occupancy drops, the operator may have fewer residents paying rent while still being responsible for its agreement with the property owner. That is a sharper risk for a company that signs the lease and then sublets rooms than for a marketplace that introduces renters to a landlord.

Contemporary reporting connected Quarters’ expansion plans with the strain that followed. Handelsblatt’s coverage described plans for large portfolio growth and the company’s withdrawal from the United States. The later bankruptcy covered ten U.S. entities, while Quarters said its European operations were unaffected. The evidence supports a market-specific lesson: a portfolio can grow faster than its lease base can be tested, and one country’s business can fail even if the parent still has active properties elsewhere.

The U.S. business ended in January

Quarters said its U.S. business closure took effect on January 15, 2021. Its U.S. entities filed for Chapter 7, a liquidation process, and the company returned American leases to landlords. Real Asset Insight’s contemporary report quoted Quarters’ statement that the U.S. closure required a restructuring at the holding level, while operations in Germany and the Netherlands would continue.

The German parent did not vanish that week. In March 2021, insolvency proceedings for the holding and German service company were resolved through a sale to Berlin competitor Habyt. A press release from the restructuring law firm describes the restructuring and completed sale. This chronology matters: the U.S. market exit and Chapter 7 filings were real failures, while the European operation and much of the wider brand were carried forward under new ownership.

What Domi can take from this

For Domi, Quarters suggests that roommate demand and available rooms need to be measured locally before a platform opens another city. A familiar product can still depend on different landlords, lease rules, commute patterns and resident needs in each market. The platform could make the location, availability date, lease issuer, property manager and room arrangement visible at the neighborhood level, then use actual inquiries and completed matches to decide where to grow. These are hypotheses to test, not proven outcomes from Quarters’ exit.

Quarters also shows why the renter’s legal relationship should remain clear when an operator or brand changes. If Domi helps residents find a shared home, it should identify who is responsible for the lease and what happens when a property manager changes. If the business ever takes on leases itself, expansion should follow evidence that each local operation can fill rooms and support residents without relying on untested growth in another market.

The shutdown did not prove that shared housing lacked demand. It showed that a cross-border brand could not make U.S. lease obligations disappear when its American entities ran out of room to continue. A shared-living product needs local supply and resident trust, but an operating company must also keep each market financially separate enough that one failed portfolio does not obscure the status of another.

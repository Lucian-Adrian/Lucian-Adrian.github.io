The Collective made shared housing feel like a building-wide service

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 5 min read
The Collective · 2021Copy link

The Collective approached co-living as more than a furnished room with housemates. It built large residences around private sleeping space and a wide range of shared facilities: kitchens, gyms, cinemas, work areas, pools and organized events. Its first major London project at Old Oak Common opened in 2016, with hundreds of rooms. The model spoke to people who wanted a home and a ready-made social setting in a city where rent and isolation could both feel punishing. The difficulty was that buildings and amenities cost money whether the rooms were full or not.

A high-service version of shared housing

The Collective sold the idea of community as part of the living experience. Residents could have a private room without furnishing an entire apartment, while using spaces that would be expensive to maintain in an ordinary flatshare. Its large buildings enabled shared facilities that smaller operators could not easily provide. The company also had a real early signal: the Old Oak Common development was completed in May 2016 and fully let by October of that year, according to a London planning document.

That success encouraged ambition. The company’s first two London projects, Old Oak Common and Canary Wharf, contained 546 and 705 rooms respectively, according to Bisnow’s account of the administration. It also pursued projects in the United States and Ireland and told investors it hoped to build toward a 100,000-unit portfolio. Large-scale communal living was the point: amenities, events and services could be spread across many residents if occupancy stayed high.

There were useful decisions in that approach. The company designed shared spaces as a core part of the product, rather than treating them as unused corners. It recognized that people moving to a new city might want privacy and community at once. Its flagship showed that some renters would choose a managed residential environment with extensive amenities. A successful building, however, did not guarantee that the next one could support the same capital structure.

Debt left little room for empty rooms

In February 2020, just before lockdowns began in the UK and United States, the company obtained a £140 million debt facility from Deutsche Bank and GCP. It partly refinanced existing borrowing and enabled the company to buy majority control of Old Oak Common. The loan also gave the management company capacity to acquire additional sites. At that point, the expansion thesis depended on occupancy and fee income continuing to support debt service.

The pandemic cut into that assumption. Lower occupancy reduced the management income available to pay interest and operating costs. A large residential building cannot turn off its shared spaces, staffing needs or debt service as quickly as residents can change their plans. According to Bisnow’s September 2021 reporting, the company fell into administration after reduced occupancy left it short of the income needed to service liabilities. The UK Companies House record confirms that The Collective (Living) Limited entered administration on September 15, 2021; the case ended on September 23, 2023. The official insolvency record makes the corporate status and dates clear.

Administration did not instantly close every residence

The distinction between the operator’s insolvency and the buildings’ status matters. The administrators said the two operating UK properties, Old Oak Common and Canary Wharf, would stay open and accept bookings with funding from their lenders. The U.S. properties sat in separate entities and remained under their directors’ control at that point. The Collective’s brand, management company, debt and property assets were connected, but they were not all one legal asset that shut down on the same day. The company’s administration notice names the UK group entities and the appointed joint administrators; the company’s insolvency process later concluded in 2023.

This makes The Collective a useful case in business structure, not only in occupancy. A premium co-living operator can run properties that remain open through a restructuring, while its corporate group is under administration and lenders decide what to sell. A resident may still have a home, but the service, management, ownership and brand around that home can change. The operating company failed to meet its financial obligations; the buildings did not all disappear.

What Domi can take from this

For Domi, The Collective suggests a product question: which parts of shared living should be validated in software before anyone commits to large physical projects? The platform can test whether people find compatible roommates, accept particular room layouts, and value cleaning, events or shared work areas before a developer finances a building around those assumptions. These are hypotheses to test with residents and property partners, not proven conclusions from one company’s failure.

The other lesson is to show who owns and operates each part of a home. If a property changes hands or an operator enters administration, residents need to know whether their lease, rent destination, maintenance contact and shared facilities remain the same. A building’s legal entity, owner, operator and listing brand may differ. Clear records and a straightforward handoff process could help residents make decisions when one of those parties changes.

The Collective demonstrated that community amenities can be a meaningful housing offer. It also showed the risk of financing a high-service building network on the expectation that occupancy and capital will remain available. For a discovery product, the practical opportunity is to make shared living easier to evaluate and coordinate while letting local demand prove itself before the physical footprint grows.

Chengcheng Find Home broke the chain between landlords and renters

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 4 min read
Chengcheng Find Home (城城找房) · 2020Copy link

In August 2020, a renter in Hainan found a room through Chengcheng Find Home and paid a year’s rent up front. Three months later, he said, the utilities were cut and his landlord put him out. His experience appeared in a follow-up report from National Business Daily in March 2021, months after Chengcheng’s network had started failing. The date matters: August was when this renter joined, not a verified shutdown date. Reports of missed rent and closed offices surfaced in October 2020. Chengcheng Find Home, the rental brand of Chengcheng Real Estate Management, had turned a housing service into a chain of promises that residents and property owners could not easily see through. The follow-up report names the brand and describes the renter’s account.

A managed home with one point of contact

Chengcheng Find Home offered renters an apartment through a company-managed contract. The operator signed management or lease agreements with property owners, then placed residents in those homes under its own service contracts. A renter could pay the company and deal with its staff instead of coordinating separately with a landlord. The pitch was practical: one booking, a ready-to-use home and support through a local branch.

It also gave the operator a difficult job. Chengcheng was responsible for keeping each side of the agreement current. In Dalian, a 2020 report described renters paying a year in advance while the operator paid owners monthly. It also found that some discounted rents to tenants fell below the amount due to the landlord once promotions were included. Dalian Evening News’ report documented this structure through interviews with renters, owners and former employees. The convenience was real, but it depended on Chengcheng maintaining enough cash and staff to keep every lease working.

A rent delay became a housing problem

By October, residents and owners in several cities were reporting overdue rent, unanswered calls and shuttered offices. In Dalian, renters had paid for longer stays, but some landlords had gone two months without payment. One household feared losing heat as winter approached. Staff told reporters they had left after missing pay. The problem reached beyond bookkeeping: when an operator stopped transferring rent, a renter could face pressure to leave even after paying the company.

Chengcheng’s own public response, quoted in the Dalian report, said the company was still operating and would address losses. It blamed pandemic pressure, owner and renter withdrawals, and panic following failures elsewhere in the sector for its cash-flow problems. That is the company’s explanation, not an independently verified account of the full cause. The reporting supports a narrower conclusion: the operator had taken on obligations to both sides, and by October some branches could not keep up with payments or service.

The legal record confirms the long tail

The disruption did not end when branches closed. In one Hangzhou case, an owner had contracted with Chengcheng’s local branch in 2019. The court record says the company had already missed two months of rent in early 2020, agreed to repay those arrears in installments, then stopped paying the rent and repayment plan from September. The owner recovered the apartment in November 2021. In March 2022, the Xiaoshan District People’s Court ordered the branch and parent company to pay overdue rent and a penalty. The published judgment is one case, not a complete account of the company’s liabilities.

By March 2021, National Business Daily was still hearing from renters seeking refunds and owners pursuing claims. One renter said he had paid for a year but lived in the home for only three months. The article reported that police were reviewing the company’s funds before deciding whether to open a criminal investigation; it did not establish a criminal finding. The report shows how a short rental-service failure could leave disputes unresolved well after the immediate crisis. Available sources document an operational collapse, but do not confirm a formal bankruptcy or a single legal dissolution date.

What a housing service should make visible

Chengcheng understood a useful customer need: renters value a simple path into a home, and owners value help finding and managing tenants. The failure was in how little visibility each side had once the operator stood between them. Paying Chengcheng did not show a renter that the landlord had received rent. Nor did an owner’s agreement with the company make the renter’s own payment safe if the service disappeared.

For Domi, the lesson is to make the responsibilities and payment state of each party visible. A resident should be able to see who controls the lease, who holds the deposit, when rent is due to the owner and what happens if an operator stops responding. Owners and renters need a reliable contact path and a documented handoff plan. A platform can simplify the relationship, but it should not hide whether the underlying obligations have been met.

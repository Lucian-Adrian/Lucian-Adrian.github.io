Common scaled shared apartments, then its U.S. operator went into liquidation

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 5 min read
Common · 2024Copy link

Common set out to make shared housing feel like a managed rental rather than an improvised roommate arrangement. Founded in New York in 2015, it furnished apartments, matched renters to private rooms, and bundled practical services into a single monthly experience. That responded to a recognizable problem: renters wanted flexibility and lower costs, but arranging furniture, utilities, roommates and repairs could make a move exhausting. Common grew into a large operator. It also became a case study in the difference between a renter-facing brand that feels simple and a property operation that remains expensive behind the scenes.

A more organized version of roommates

Common’s offer combined private bedrooms with shared kitchens and living rooms, often with furniture, utilities, cleaning and community features included. It presented a familiar form of housing in a more consistent package. A renter did not need to furnish a whole apartment or recruit friends before moving. The company also expanded beyond co-living into microunits and traditional apartments, giving it more than one way to reach renters and property owners.

The business grew quickly. In January 2023, Common and Germany-based Habyt announced a merger to form a global operator. Their release said the combined group would span more than 40 cities and 14 countries, and that Common had over $110 million in venture investment. The announcement described Common as managing thousands of units across co-living, microunit and conventional apartments. The merger announcement shows how the company framed scale and international reach as its next advantage.

That growth brought real capability. A sizable portfolio gave Common more operating experience, a recognizable brand and the chance to reuse leasing and resident-service processes across buildings. It also gave landlords a single operator for shared housing that might otherwise be difficult to manage. But each new city required local staff, property relationships and a steady stream of renters. A large unit count did not remove those costs.

The portfolio expanded faster than its obligations shrank

Common expanded from roughly 2,000 to 7,000 units between 2020 and 2022, according to Bisnow’s report on the bankruptcy. It also acquired or took on properties after other co-living operators faltered. Expansion increased the number of buildings and residents the company could serve, but it meant carrying a much larger service network while rents, staffing and financing costs were changing.

On May 31, 2024, Common Living, Inc. filed for Chapter 7 bankruptcy in Delaware. That is a liquidation proceeding. The filing reported assets of up to $10 million and liabilities of up to $50 million, as summarized by Bloomberg Law. The filing followed the company’s 2023 merger with Habyt; it was the U.S. Common operating entity that entered bankruptcy, while the wider Habyt group continued. That distinction matters. The co-living operator had failed as a stand-alone U.S. business, while some buildings and resident arrangements could continue with other managers.

The causes were layered. Contemporary reporting connected the shutdown to rapid expansion, high operating overhead and rising interest rates. Co-living uses real buildings, staff and leases or management contracts, so it can inherit costs long before occupancy stabilizes. The pandemic had already disrupted some shared-housing demand. Financing can extend the runway, but it cannot make a building profitable if its occupancy, pricing and service costs do not work together.

A renter’s convenience still depends on the property agreement

Common made moving into a shared apartment easier to understand and easier to arrange. Yet convenience depended on a chain of agreements among residents, the operating company, building owners and lenders. If the operator failed, the renter still needed to know who controlled the property, who held the lease and deposit, where rent should go, and who would respond to repairs.

This is the operational edge that a polished product can hide. The company might own a brand and coordinate service without owning the homes. A smooth app experience cannot settle legal or practical questions when the intermediary disappears. Common’s filing also demonstrates why reporting a combined group’s scale is different from reporting which entity signs a lease or owes an obligation. For renters, that boundary is concrete. For founders, it belongs in the product design and the contracts.

What Domi can take from this

For Domi, Common suggests a set of hypotheses to test, not proven product answers. Roommate discovery may work best when the platform makes the responsibilities around the match just as clear as the profile. Each listing could show who the legal landlord is, who manages the home, what payment goes to which party, how deposits are handled, and who remains responsible if a manager changes. That information could help roommates distinguish a matching service from a property operator.

The expansion lesson is to grow neighborhood by neighborhood. A marketplace can measure whether enough compatible renters and real, current listings exist before adding more regions. It can also make renter support and property handoffs part of the service promise. If Domi chooses to operate properties later, it should test local occupancy, service costs, owner contracts and payment flow before multiplying leases. The important question is not only how many rooms are listed. It is whether a renter can trust the relationship to continue when a company changes.

Danke grew a rental network faster than its cash could support

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 5 min read
Danke Apartment · 2020Copy link

Danke Apartment tried to make city renting feel more dependable. It took ordinary apartments, furnished them to a consistent standard, divided many into private rooms, and added digital locks and resident support. By 2019, the operator said it was running more than 400,000 units across 13 Chinese cities. One year later, landlords reported missed payments, tenants faced eviction, and new listings disappeared from Danke’s app. The platform’s public collapse followed an operating model in which housing commitments and cash receipts moved on different clocks.

Standardizing the shared apartment

Phoenix Tree Holdings launched Danke in 2015 for young urban renters who wanted a furnished room without having to arrange furniture, maintenance, or household services themselves. The company leased homes from individual owners, renovated them, then rented rooms or whole apartments to residents. It also introduced a separate dormitory-style brand for corporate clients. Its 2019 prospectus described cleaning, repairs, Wi-Fi, and round-the-clock resident support as part of the package. The model brought property, furnishing, and service into one renter-facing product. Phoenix Tree’s SEC filing lays out the company’s service and its growth from 2,434 units in 2015 to 438,309 at the end of 2019.

That ambition made sense in cities where rents were high and renters had little time or cash to set up a home. A standardized apartment could make room search simpler, give tenants a predictable point of contact, and let landlords reach residents without furnishing each unit themselves. Danke also invested in software to help source apartments and manage operations. Technology could reduce coordination costs, but it could not remove the underlying need to furnish units, keep them occupied, and pay owners on time.

The rent calendar became a financing strategy

Danke signed leases with property owners that generally lasted four to six years, while tenant agreements usually lasted about a year. In some arrangements, tenants took out a loan for a year of rent, and the lending partner paid Danke up front while the tenant repaid the loan monthly. Caixin reported that landlords were often paid monthly or quarterly, and that Danke had used the earlier cash to support expansion. That structure made an apartment network easier to scale while tenants had a lower immediate payment burden. It also meant the company’s cash position depended on continuing access to new renters and financing.

The key risk was timing. The company owed landlords under longer commitments, paid the costs of renovation and service, and relied on tenancy and prepayments to keep cash circulating. If new leasing slowed or a landlord refused a rent reduction, the operator still had bills. Caixin reported that landlords were sometimes charging less to tenants than the rent Danke owed owners, a spread that could make expansion increase losses rather than earnings. Its reporting also cited a first-quarter 2020 net loss of $174.3 million, which the company attributed in part to the pandemic. Caixin’s investigation of the cash crisis and its report on Danke seeking a buyer describe the financing and operating pressures.

The platform stopped matching homes to renters

The pandemic weakened demand and made the company’s cash pool harder to replenish. By late 2020, landlords complained that they had not been paid, while residents who had paid or borrowed for rent still faced demands to leave. South China Morning Post reporting documented landlords cancelling contracts after Danke failed to pay and tenants being caught between the operator and property owners. On December 25, the newspaper reported that the app had stopped displaying vacant homes, prices, and other listing details. That was evidence of a platform operating in distress, not proof that every rental unit or legal entity had instantly disappeared. SCMP’s reporting on the landlord and tenant disputes and the app’s suspended listings captures that distinction.

The financial strain also became a disclosure problem. NYSE Regulation suspended trading in Phoenix Tree’s shares in March 2021 and began delisting proceedings the next month, citing missing requested information, late filings, and inadequate or inaccurate disclosure. The exchange action does not by itself explain the rental business’s failure, but it shows that the crisis had reached the company’s ability to report to investors. The NYSE notice documents the listing action.

The causal interpretation is not that technology was useless. Standardized rooms, service requests, and rental data could improve the experience. The problem was building a large, cash-intensive operator whose promised convenience depended on favorable occupancy, rent spreads, and fresh financing at the same time. The network grew faster than the company proved it could fund each home through a full rental cycle.

What Domi should carry forward

For Domi, the practical lesson is to distinguish a verified, available home from a record in a database. A large inventory number does not tell a renter whether the owner has authorized a lease, whether the room is occupied, or who will actually receive rent. Each offer should show its current status, the responsible party, and the conditions for payment and deposit return.

Danke also shows why payment flows should not be used to mask weak unit economics. If a housing service accepts money, it needs a clear view of what is owed to the renter, owner, and lender, and when. If Domi acts only as a matching platform, that boundary should be explicit. If a future service takes on management or payment duties, it should prove that the arrangement works at the level of an individual home before multiplying the same obligation across cities. Those are analytical lessons from Danke’s reported structure, not claims about Domi’s current operations.

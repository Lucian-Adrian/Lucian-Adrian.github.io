Dingjia’s bankruptcy showed what happens when rent is borrowed in advance

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 5 min read
Dingjia · 2018Copy link

Dingjia entered Hangzhou’s long-term rental market with the familiar promise of a furnished apartment and a lower monthly payment. The arrangement looked manageable to a renter who could spread rent over time. Behind the scenes, however, some tenants’ annual rent was financed through a loan that paid the operator in advance. When Dingjia stopped operating in August 2018, tenants could still owe the lender while landlords said they had not received rent. The bankruptcy exposed how a convenience product can transfer risk instead of removing it.

A managed apartment for a growing city

Dingjia began renting long-term apartments under its Dingyu brand in 2017, according to Zhejiang Online. It sourced properties from owners, furnished and renovated them, and then rented rooms or apartments to residents. In February 2018, the company announced a pre-Series A investment from a single backer. The brief expansion story looked familiar: capital would help it add homes, while a branded service would make it easier for tenants to find a place and owners to earn rent.

The model addressed real renter needs. A furnished room lowers the effort required to move, and smaller monthly payments can make a home accessible to a worker who cannot pay several months of rent at once. A rental operator can also give owners a tenant-facing contact for maintenance and renewals. The idea had value, but the company needed stable occupancy and enough margin to cover both home acquisition and service.

A loan made rent look more affordable

In some reported cases, a tenant was told they could make monthly payments, while the payment flow involved a third-party rent loan. The lender could pay Dingjia a larger sum at once, and the tenant repaid the lender over time. The payment lowered the amount due immediately, but it also meant the tenant’s debt could survive a dispute with the rental operator. A homeowner might receive rent from Dingjia on a different schedule from the lender’s payment to Dingjia.

People’s Daily, carrying reporting from Huashang, described tenants who said they had been signed into loans to fund their rentals and remained responsible for repayments after Dingjia failed. That wording matters: the article reports tenants’ experiences and industry criticism, not a court finding that every contract had been concealed. It reported about 4,000 affected tenants. Zhejiang Online separately cited the company’s bankruptcy notice and said roughly 1,950 landlords could face losses. The People’s Daily report and Zhejiang Online’s follow-up document the two sides of the dispute.

Expansion consumed the money the model needed

On August 20, 2018, Dingjia announced that poor business management had led to a broken funding chain and that it had stopped operating. The company entered bankruptcy liquidation. In a follow-up interview, its chairman attributed the collapse to overexpansion, high vacancy, and the cost of taking on too many apartments. The company had reportedly been charging tenants less than the cost of securing the homes from owners. That is a cash problem as much as a pricing problem: rent collected from a tenant could not cover a higher owner payment, renovation, and operating costs for the full lease term.

The reporting also describes how the advance from rent loans could encourage an operator to keep adding inventory. A one-year payment received at the start can make current cash appear strong, but it is matched by a continuing obligation to house a tenant, pay an owner, and service any loan arrangement. If a home remains vacant or the operator expands faster than it can fill and maintain units, the money already collected is not free cash. It has future obligations attached to it.

The business failure was therefore not one bad month or one failed apartment. The cost base grew with every lease. The company’s own account emphasized poor management and liquidity, while independent coverage described tenant loans, landlord arrears, and thousands of affected residents. Economic Observer’s report on the financing partner adds detail on how annual rent loans were paid to Dingjia and later repaid by tenants.

A cheaper first payment needs a safe second step

The good intention behind a monthly rental plan is easy to understand. Tenants may not have enough savings for a large deposit and months of rent at once. Credit can bridge that gap when the renter understands the agreement and the housing operator remains able to deliver the home. But a loan does not make rent cheaper. It moves the payment across time and adds a lender whose claim remains even if the apartment contract breaks down.

Dingjia’s core error, as the reporting supports it, was failing to balance the promise of lower immediate costs with the operator’s own lease burden and ability to pay owners. It expanded by committing to apartments before the cash and occupancy could support them. Residents then inherited a difficult contract triangle: renter, rental company, and lender could each point to a different agreement.

What Domi should carry forward

Domi can make rental offers safer by showing the full payment path before a tenant signs. If a loan or instalment service is involved, the tenant should be able to identify the lender, total repayment, cancellation rules, and what happens if the operator stops providing the room. Rent, deposits, and loan payments should not be presented as one vague monthly amount.

The same clarity matters in roommate arrangements. Each roommate should know whether they are tenants on the owner’s lease, subtenants under a lead renter, or customers of a property manager. Domi can surface the distinction and keep signed agreements, payment receipts, and deposit responsibility attached to the actual home. That is an analytical lesson from Dingjia’s reported collapse: affordability is real only when the obligations stay understandable after the platform is no longer in the middle.

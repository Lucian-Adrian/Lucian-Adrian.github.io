Homigo and the cost of losing trust between a landlord and a roommate

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 5 min read
Homigo · 2019Copy link

Homigo set out to solve a familiar first-week problem in Bengaluru: a newcomer needed a furnished place to live, but finding a room also meant negotiating deposits, furniture, utilities, and unfamiliar landlords. The company packaged those jobs into a co-living offer. By March 2019, that convenience had unravelled into unanswered calls, disputed deposits, and residents being asked to leave homes they believed they had rented. The record supports an operational collapse, though it does not establish a formal liquidation or a criminal conviction.

A furnished room as a product

Co-founder Nikunj Batheja developed the idea after arriving in the city and struggling to find a furnished home. Homigo launched in 2015 with his batchmate Jatin Mitruka, and later added Akash Verma as a founder. It leased or managed properties, furnished them, and offered rooms, beds, or whole homes to young renters. That meant a tenant could search for a ready-to-move-in room through one company instead of coordinating separately with a landlord, furniture supplier, and housemates. YourStory’s March 2019 account describes the company’s origin and model.

The product addressed a real friction. A renter who moves for work often wants predictable costs, basic furnishings, and a room available on a known date. Homigo also had a potential advantage over a bare listing site: if it controlled the property experience, it could standardize the room and support residents after move-in. The company raised about $200,000 from investors, including home-furnishing businesses Mebelkart and Livspace, according to YourStory. Its opportunity was practical, but the service required reliable work behind every listing.

The operating burden behind each listing

Homigo’s model tied together obligations that arrived on different schedules. It had to secure homes from owners, prepare them for residents, maintain the property, and collect rent and deposits. A furnished room could not be delivered through software alone. Someone had to pay the owner, repair a broken appliance, keep utilities working, and return a deposit when a tenant left.

This created a working-capital test. Rent and owner obligations did not disappear when a bed remained empty or a new tenant delayed move-in. Furnishing and maintenance costs arrived before the company could recover them from residents. More homes could expand the addressable market, but they also multiplied the number of payments and service promises the company had to meet. The inference from the reporting is that Homigo’s product ambition grew faster than its ability to fund and supervise that physical operation.

YourStory reported that Homigo had expanded across Bengaluru and received investor funding, while both revenue and expenses had risen. In February 2019, the company emailed residents that it had temporarily halted all Bengaluru operations for two months because it lacked funds and wanted to stop losses while seeking additional money. The message asked tenants to remain calm. That explanation framed the halt as a pause, but it did not resolve who would pay landlords or return deposits.

A temporary halt became a housing crisis

By March, residents told reporters that landlords were demanding possession because they had not received rent from Homigo. YourStory said it could not independently confirm some reports of unpaid rent, so that part of the story remained contested at the time. The complaints themselves, however, were concrete: tenants said they had not received deposits, had been told to vacate, or could no longer reach the company.

The Times of India reported that police had registered complaints from tenants, property owners, and staff, and that investigators were looking into allegations against the company and its directors. The New Indian Express reported that more than 3,000 residents across Bengaluru were affected, that the company had leased over 100 properties, and that some tenants were given no clear answer about whether they could remain. These are reports of complaints and an investigation, not findings that establish the founders’ guilt. The Times of India and The New Indian Express documented the claims and the disruption.

An acquisition was discussed but did not rescue the operation. Nestaway told YourStory it had held initial discussions, completed checks, and decided against acquiring Homigo. The possible transfer of hundreds of beds was not a completed sale. That distinction matters: a conversation about a buyer does not mean the company’s rental obligations or customer claims were taken over.

What Domi should carry forward

Homigo’s collapse shows that a room listing is only credible if the renter can see who controls the home and who is responsible after the key handover. For Domi, the useful design lesson is to make the rental chain explicit: identify the property owner, the leaseholder, the service operator, and the party receiving rent. Deposit rules and return dates should be visible before a renter commits, and a tenant should know whom to contact if the operator stops responding.

The same lesson applies to roommate matching. A successful introduction does not prove that a room is available, that the lease permits another occupant, or that the person collecting money can legally offer it. Domi can build trust by attaching clear evidence to each offer and recording whether a listing is merely proposed, verified with its owner, or ready for a signed agreement. That is a product inference from Homigo’s reported failure: keeping the contract boundary legible matters as much as making the search easy.

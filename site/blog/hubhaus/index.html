HubHaus left shared-home residents and landlords with the same unanswered question

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 5 min read
HubHaus · 2020Copy link

HubHaus tried to turn the shared house into a reliable housing product. It leased large homes, furnished common rooms and rented individual bedrooms to working professionals. Residents could move into a ready-made household without finding every roommate themselves, buying furniture or setting up utilities from scratch. Homeowners, in turn, were promised steady rent and a professional manager. The model addressed two real frustrations at once. Its failure showed how quickly those promises could become liabilities when a middle company could no longer pay the people on either side of the arrangement.

The startup around the group house

Founded in 2016, HubHaus presented co-living as a way to make urban housing more attainable while also helping newcomers find community. Its online tools handled applications and resident requests, and the company offered services such as cleaning and Wi-Fi. A group house was familiar, but HubHaus tried to make the experience more turnkey and consistent. The renter could focus on a bedroom and housemates, while the homeowner could work with one operator instead of managing a rotating group of tenants.

That service wrapped several responsibilities together. HubHaus signed leases with property owners, then sublet rooms to residents. The company had to fill enough bedrooms, collect enough rent and manage common areas while still paying the owner according to its own agreement. This structure could make moving simpler for renters and income more predictable for property owners. It also meant the company carried obligations even when a bedroom was empty or a resident left early.

A cash interruption moved through the whole chain

In June 2020, The Information reported that HubHaus was trying to find a buyer or emergency funding after struggling to raise another round. By September, the company had laid off its staff and told homeowners and tenants that a closure and liquidation process would begin on September 23. Its email said it could not pay October rent and asked landlords to apply security deposits toward it. The company also said residents’ leases would transfer to the homeowners. The San Francisco Chronicle’s reporting documented the notices, the funding history and the transfer plan.

The transition was not merely a corporate event. When HubHaus stopped paying owners, homeowners had to decide whether to take over the rent relationship, while tenants had to understand whether they could stay and whom to contact. VICE’s report described claims that some residents kept being charged for services that were no longer provided and that landlords were not receiving full rent. It also quoted a liquidation letter saying unsecured creditors would not be paid. Those accounts make clear that the breakdown affected people who had already done their part by paying.

The company blamed the pandemic’s severe effect on housing. A contemporary interview with the CEO of another Boston co-living startup, PlaceMe, described renters leaving the city and revenue falling sharply in spring 2020. That is useful market context, but it is not a measured account of HubHaus’s own occupancy decline. The evidence specific to HubHaus is that it could not meet its rent obligations, could not pay October rent and entered liquidation. The fixed commitments in a master-lease model made a sudden demand shock especially dangerous.

Convenience rested on a fragile intermediary

HubHaus was more than a roommate-matching website. It controlled leases, rent collection, furnishings and operations. That can give residents a more coordinated experience when the operator is healthy. It can also concentrate risk: if the intermediary disappears, the person who paid rent may not know whether the landlord has received it, and the homeowner may have an occupied property without the expected payment.

This risk was made worse by the distance between tenant payments and the owner’s rent. A company’s cash problems could show up first as missed utility bills or service gaps, then as an inability to pay the master rent. The San Francisco Chronicle reported that HubHaus proposed using security deposits to cover the October amount due to homeowners. That does not establish what happened to every individual payment, but it shows how an operator with little reserve can transfer the shock to residents and owners at once.

What Domi can take from this

For Domi, HubHaus raises a concrete product question: what must a roommate finder show when it is not the legal landlord? That answer should be visible before a renter pays. A listing could identify the property owner, the lease issuer, the payment recipient, the repair contact and how a lease survives a management change. If Domi handles payments, the ledger should make clear when money is received, who it is held for and when it is passed to the owner. Those are hypotheses to test with renters and landlords, not claims that interface design alone prevents a business failure.

The second question is whether Domi should operate properties or remain a matching and discovery service. Operating can improve coordination, but it also adds long-term rent obligations and responsibility for utilities, maintenance and occupancy. A platform model may be lighter, provided it does not obscure who has legal responsibility. HubHaus shows that a convenient roommate experience depends on a sound arrangement behind the screen. When that arrangement breaks, the people sharing the house need a transition plan before they need a new login.

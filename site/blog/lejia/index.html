Lejia’s reverse rent spread turned growth into a liability

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 5 min read
Lejia Apartment · 2019Copy link

Lejia Apartment sold a simple promise: a renter could move into a furnished place without arranging every detail from scratch. To deliver that promise, the Nanjing operator signed long leases with property owners, then sublet homes to individual tenants. In August 2019, after weeks of public statements that operations were normal, Lejia said it had no operating income and could not repay its debts. It closed its business. The failure was unusually legible because the company’s own account described a pricing model that made growth increasingly costly.

A landlord-facing business with a tenant-facing brand

Lejia began around 2016 and grew rapidly in the Chinese long-term rental market. By the time it shut down, reporting said it had reached eight cities, roughly 100,000 listings at its peak, and a workforce of more than 1,000. Those figures describe the scale it had advertised or reached, not proof that every listing was leased or occupied. Its basic service was more operational than a marketplace: secure homes from landlords, prepare them, and place renters into a branded, furnished experience. Mingtiandi’s report on the shutdown summarized the company’s footprint and its lease-and-sublet model.

The service addressed a real gap. A furnished home can save a renter time and upfront spending, especially when moving to a new city. Lejia also offered owners a single operator to manage the home instead of finding tenants themselves. In a healthy version of the model, standard furnishings, maintenance, and a larger tenant pool can create value for both sides. That depended on Lejia getting the price and occupancy assumptions right for each neighborhood.

The rent spread moved in the wrong direction

Lejia’s closure notice said the business model had serious defects and that the company lacked internal management systems. Yicai Global reported that landlords questioned a pattern in which Lejia paid more to secure homes than it charged tenants to rent them. The arithmetic left little room for vacancies, furnishing costs, repairs, or late rent. If a room sat empty, the company continued to carry the owner’s rent while receiving nothing from a tenant.

That is a different risk from a listing platform that simply introduces an owner and renter and charges a fee. Lejia took on the home itself. Every new lease could add a future payment obligation before the company knew how quickly it could fill the unit. A large listing count could look like momentum while each occupied room still lost money. The analytical inference is that Lejia treated market presence as a substitute for a positive contribution from each home. Expansion magnified the gap instead of closing it.

A sudden reversal left little time to recover

The end came quickly. Lejia issued a statement on July 21, 2019, saying its business was operating normally. Less than three weeks later, its August 7 notice said it had stopped operations, closed all business, and dismissed most staff. The notice acknowledged no operating income and debts it could not repay. Yicai Global reported that the company had been unable to pay debts and had faced landlord and tenant complaints even before the announcement. Its August 9 account describes the reversal; Mingtiandi also reported the company’s formal closure statement.

The shutdown created a double disruption. Owners had to regain control of apartments and find new renters. Residents who had paid the operator still had to negotiate with owners whose payments were in arrears. Yicai’s Chinese-language follow-up reported that tenants and owners faced the consequences of the operator’s closure, including rent claims and difficulty contacting the company. Those outcomes are consistent with a business whose customer relationship depended on Lejia remaining solvent throughout the tenancy.

The useful idea and the broken assumption

Lejia was trying to improve an everyday process. A renter could search for a furnished room under a recognizable brand, and an owner could outsource preparation and tenant handling. The approach can work when the operator knows the real cost of acquiring, furnishing, and maintaining a home, then prices the unit to cover those costs under ordinary vacancy and repair conditions.

Lejia’s reported reverse rent spread undermined that value proposition. The company needed not only more renters but better unit economics: either lower acquisition costs, higher rents justified by service, or more efficient operations. The reporting does not show that any single one of those changes could have saved it. It does show that scale without sustainable pricing left it unable to meet the basic promise of a rental operator: the home would remain available and someone would answer for the contract.

What Domi should carry forward

For Domi, the first lesson is to distinguish a listing from a home that has been confirmed and priced for a real agreement. Inventory figures should not mix active, available homes with unverified or unavailable units. A renter needs to know whether the person offering a room can authorize it and whether the rent reflects a current owner agreement.

The second lesson is to keep each participant’s responsibilities visible. When Domi connects roommates or renters without taking a lease, it should say so clearly and show who receives rent, who returns the deposit, and who handles repairs. If it later manages homes directly, its growth should be measured by retained, successfully served tenancies and sustainable unit-level economics, not by listings alone. That conclusion is an interpretation of Lejia’s reported pricing problem, not a claim that the company disclosed a complete cost ledger.

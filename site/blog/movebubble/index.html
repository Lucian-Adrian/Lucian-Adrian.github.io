Movebubble put renters first, then its rental app went offline

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 5 min read
Movebubble · 2022Copy link

For a renter, a listing that is already taken is not a small inconvenience. It can mean another call, another viewing, and another evening spent trying to understand a city that still feels unfamiliar. Movebubble built its business around that frustration. The London startup aimed to combine property search, current availability, agent contact and viewing bookings in one place, with renters contributing their own impressions of buildings and neighborhoods. In February 2022, the app and website went offline. Companies House records the company entering creditors’ voluntary liquidation that May and being dissolved in October 2024.

A renter-first response to a familiar problem

Movebubble was founded in 2013 by Aidan Rushby, Tony Edwards and Logan Hall. The founders saw that renters were often asked to search across property sites, call agents one by one, and keep their own notes about appointments and areas. Its app tried to make those disconnected steps feel like one service. It listed rentals, let users book viewings, showed area guides and encouraged renters to share feedback. A key promise was that listings would reflect real-time availability, so a search result would be more likely to lead to a home a renter could actually view. TechCrunch’s 2016 profile described the product and reported that a further $1.6 million round brought announced investment to just over $3.4 million.

That was a real product insight. A rental search is not just a database problem. The renter is coordinating with other people, deciding which neighborhoods might work, and acting under a deadline. Movebubble tried to improve the entire search-and-viewing journey rather than simply display more properties. That distinction made its product easier to explain and gave it opportunities to collect useful feedback from renters.

Search still depended on someone else’s inventory

The strongest part of the product also created a difficult dependency. Movebubble could improve how a renter viewed listings, but the homes and availability information still came from landlords and agents. In 2016, TechCrunch noted that a user could not yet complete a rental through the app. Even its real-time availability promise depended on timely information from the people controlling each home. If an agent did not update a listing, or did not respond to a viewing request, a polished app could not complete the job alone.

The company also had to serve more than one side of the market. Renters wanted a broad and accurate choice of homes. Agents needed a reason to provide dependable inventory and respond quickly. A useful search interface could attract renters, but activity on its own did not guarantee a dependable supply of current listings or revenue. The balance required local relationships and operational follow-through as well as software.

The product disappeared before the explanation did

By 14 February 2022, Movebubble’s site displayed a notice about technical problems that were taking longer than expected to fix. Its app was offline, and the company did not respond to enquiries from reporters. Property Industry Eye reported that the platform had focused mainly on London and Manchester, raised more than £2 million through Seedrs, and had recently seen director departures. It also noted that a compulsory strike-off had been suspended. Those details show warning signs, but they do not establish one definitive cause of failure.

The legal end is clearer. Companies House records show that Movebubble Limited entered creditors’ voluntary liquidation on 4 May 2022 and was dissolved on 3 October 2024. The public record does not include a founder’s postmortem explaining the decision. It is more accurate to say the service failed and the company was liquidated than to assign the shutdown to one unverified cause.

Several pressures are visible in the design. A renter-first experience raised expectations for accurate, current supply. That experience depended on agents and landlords maintaining data and responding. Movebubble also needed enough market coverage to be useful while building the relationships that made the information dependable. These are structural challenges, not proof of what caused the final technical outage. A product can be thoughtful and still be fragile if the people, data and operations behind the interface cannot keep pace.

What Domi can take from this

For Domi, Movebubble is a reason to make freshness and completion visible. A large collection of source ads is not the same thing as homes confirmed available today. Each listing should retain its source and observation date, and the experience should make uncertainty clear. Domi can measure whether a renter reaches a real inquiry, gets a response, books a viewing and finds a home, instead of treating clicks or saved listings as proof that search works.

It also suggests a focused local launch. Domi can first test whether a small area has enough current listings and responsive operators to help a clearly defined group of renters. If information comes from an owner or agent, the product can show who supplied it and when it was last checked. If a listing becomes unavailable, the app should let the user know and make the next action clear. Those practices build trust while Domi tests its marketplace. They do not require promising an end-to-end rental transaction before the underlying supply and response process are reliable.

OpenDoor closed its co-living business while its homes continued elsewhere

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 4 min read
OpenDoor · 2021Copy link

OpenDoor treated co-living as a set of relationships to design, not simply bedrooms to rent. The Bay Area company created furnished shared homes, screened applicants for fit with the house, and asked residents to contribute to the culture and upkeep of the community. It began in 2013 and operated for about nine years, according to its own account. In July 2021, it announced that it would close. The company said its 19 properties and 406 units would continue under new management and that residents had retained their housing. The houses outlasted the operator.

A home designed around the people in it

OpenDoor focused on people moving into a shared home, with private bedrooms and generous communal kitchens, dining and living spaces. Its team described the homes as places for skill sharing, shared meals and events. Residents were not just assigned to a room; they were asked about the kind of household they wanted and what they could contribute. In one early account, the Los Angeles Times reported that OpenDoor asked applicants about their interests and contributions, and that its homes used different ownership and operating structures. The 2016 profile gives a picture of the company’s early approach.

The company’s own property guide described systems for food sharing, chores, conflict, house meetings and new-member introductions. It treated those routines as part of the housing product. That was a useful instinct: an apartment can have shared rooms and still fail to feel like a stable home if nobody knows how to handle dishes, guests, repairs or common space. OpenDoor tried to make the practical side of group living visible rather than leaving all of it to chance.

A deliberate shutdown, with a handoff

On July 19, 2021, OpenDoor posted that it had decided to close after nine years. The notice said it had taken care to give real-estate partners time to transition management and reduce the impact on staff and residents. It reported that all residents had retained their housing and that the properties would stay under new management. The shutdown announcement is the primary record of the company’s stated decision and the outcome it described.

OpenDoor did not publish a financial explanation in that notice. There is not enough evidence to attribute the decision to a particular loss, funding gap or market event. A later property page states that the company was no longer operational and was no longer managing its homes by December 15, 2022. That reference page confirms the operating end while continuing to describe the homes and their design.

The handoff makes this a different kind of failure story from a sudden property closure. The company ended its management service; it said the homes and residents remained. That does not establish that every transition went smoothly, and the page is a company source. It does establish that the physical housing offer and the operator were separable. A resident community can continue after the company that organized it has stopped work.

What the story does and does not show

OpenDoor’s choices around house culture were distinctive. The company built resident input into onboarding and day-to-day household practices, and it described multiple approaches to financing or operating its properties rather than presenting every house as one identical deal. In 2020, a Cushman & Wakefield market report listed Open Door among operators in the U.S. market, estimating 255 current beds and a larger announced pipeline. Those were market estimates, not audited outcomes.

The decision to close can be evaluated only within that evidence. The public notice does not explain whether the company ran short of capital, chose to wind down, or found another structure preferable. It would be misleading to assign it the same financial diagnosis as a company that filed for liquidation. What can be said is that its operation depended on an organizational layer to manage the homes, and the company chose to end that layer while the properties transitioned to new managers.

What Domi can take from this

For Domi, OpenDoor suggests hypotheses about designing shared housing around resident contribution. The product could give a household clear ways to share chores, house rules, meals and events, while still letting residents choose how much they participate. Matching could consider practical preferences such as quiet hours, guests, cleaning and shared food instead of leaning only on broad personality labels. OpenDoor’s history does not prove which specific features improve retention, so these ideas would need direct testing with renters.

The other lesson is to make a home’s continuity visible. If an operator leaves, residents need to know who now manages the property, where rent goes and how to get repairs handled. Domi could show the difference between a room listing, the legal landlord and the current property manager, and keep resident-facing information current when those roles change. A company’s closure does not automatically mean every shared home has failed. The product should help people understand what changed and which relationships still hold.

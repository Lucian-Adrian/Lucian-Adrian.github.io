PocketList tried to list apartments before renters had to compete for them

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 4 min read
PocketList · 2021Copy link

Most apartment listings appear only after a home is already empty or a landlord is ready to advertise. PocketList tried to reach renters earlier. Its Los Angeles app invited people to describe the apartments they lived in, share their experiences and signal that they planned to move. Other renters could discover those homes before they appeared on public listing sites. The company launched in 2020 with ambitious plans for several cities. Less than a year later, its founders told employees and investors they were closing operations.

Renters knew about the next vacancy first

PocketList grew out of an earlier product called Block, a tool for renters looking for an apartment together. After several attempts to find a clearer concept, co-founders Nick Dazé and Julian Vergel de Dios built PocketList around a different insight: existing renters often know they are moving before the property manager knows the home will be vacant. The service asked renters to describe their apartment, rate details such as natural light and parking, and share when they expected to leave. Future renters could ask questions and contact a landlord or manager through the app.

The approach gave renters useful information before a crowded public search and gave landlords a chance to meet potential tenants earlier. TechCrunch’s launch profile reported that the app had tens of thousands of Los Angeles listings described by renters, with plans to enter San Francisco and San Diego. PocketList was free to renters and expected to charge landlords and property managers for qualified leads. At launch it was still pre-revenue.

Early engagement did not yet prove the business

PocketList grew quickly on the renter side. Dazé told dot.LA that sign-ups and engagement had nearly doubled month over month and that the app reached around 75,000 renters at its peak. The company raised a $2.8 million seed round led by Craft Ventures in 2020. Investor interest, user growth and a novel supply signal gave PocketList a credible opening.

But the revenue side still needed proof. The service depended on landlords paying for leads or tools, while renters were the ones creating much of the early value by describing homes and sharing their plans. The product’s central promise also depended on enough renters reporting accurate information and enough property operators responding to it. A large user count was encouraging, but it could not show that an apartment owner would pay consistently or that the timing advantage would convert into leases.

The pandemic cut across the launch plan

PocketList had planned to expand into San Francisco and San Diego, then later Seattle, New York and Chicago. Those rollouts never came. The company was born just as COVID-19 disrupted leasing patterns in coastal cities. Renters moved less, landlords faced new financial pressures, and interest in a new marketing tool slowed. In the dot.LA interview, Dazé said activity on PocketList slowed and landlords were less inclined to experiment with new apps.

The founders cut costs after the seed round closed in April 2020. Dazé said they reduced monthly spending by about 30 percent, ended their office lease and cancelled software subscriptions without immediately laying off staff. They also experimented with paid features and landlord pricing. Still, several large customer deals that the team had worked on fell through, and its next fundraising effort failed. In the final week of April 2021, the founders notified eight remote employees and investors that they were closing for good. Dazé said the company had not run out of cash, but did not have enough to make a major pivot. The remaining funds were to be distributed to investors based on their contributions.

The pandemic was a major shock, but the company’s pre-revenue position made that shock harder to absorb. PocketList had promising renter engagement and a good product thesis. It had not yet established repeatable landlord payments or expanded beyond its first market. That made the timing of the crisis important without making it the only explanation.

What Domi can take from this

PocketList shows why Domi should separate renter activity from marketplace validation. A renter passport, saved listing or roommate invitation can indicate interest, but the product should also measure whether the information leads to a response, a qualified inquiry, a viewing and eventually a move. For the operator side, Domi can test whether managers will pay for a specific outcome, such as less time screening inquiries or more viewings that fit a home’s requirements.

The renter-powered idea is useful as a hypothesis. Domi could let people share a move window, budget and non-negotiables with a group before a home search begins. That can help friends coordinate and make distribution more natural. But the app should be transparent about what is verified and what comes from a renter’s observation. It can start in one city, recruit a small community and test real operator participation before expanding to more markets. PocketList’s growth suggests the consumer insight was attractive; its closure shows that interest is not the same as a paying marketplace.

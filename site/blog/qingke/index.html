Qingke’s apartment operator entered bankruptcy while its listed parent continued

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 5 min read
Qingke Apartments · 2022Copy link

Qingke Apartments was among China’s early branded rental operators, promising young city residents a ready-to-move-in home with furnishings and services. Its technology-enabled model helped it grow from a local operator into a Nasdaq-listed company. The legal ending requires precision: in 2022, the Shanghai operating company Qingke Public Rental entered bankruptcy liquidation, and other operating subsidiaries followed. The listed holding company did not disappear at that moment. It later changed its name to FLJ Group and continued as a separate corporate entity.

A standardized apartment for young renters

Qingke began operating in Shanghai in 2012, according to filings by its later listed parent, Q&K International Group. The service offered furnished apartments to young professionals, with technology for sourcing homes, renovation, tenant acquisition, and property management. Its value proposition was straightforward: a renter could choose a ready-to-live-in room with fewer setup tasks, and a landlord could reach tenants through a centralized operator.

Investors saw a large market in that promise. Qingke raised capital and went public on Nasdaq in November 2019. But scale did not establish profitability. Mingtiandi, drawing on the IPO prospectus and later financial reporting, said the company had never achieved profitability and had accumulated nearly 2.8 billion yuan in losses over four years, including more than 1.5 billion yuan in 2020. The company’s own filings described a network of rental and value-added services, while also identifying competition, financing, and maintaining relationships with tenants and landlords as risks. The SEC’s 2021 annual report and Mingtiandi’s account of the bankruptcy provide different views of the same business.

Losses reached the homes behind the brand

Qingke’s problems were not limited to the stock price. Mingtiandi reported that landlords and residents had faced payment disputes, evictions, and service interruptions after owners said they had not received rent. It cited local reporting on more than a thousand administrative rulings and prior judgments that remained unpaid. These are documented legal and property disputes, not a finding that every rental agreement or property had the same issue.

The core operational challenge was that the renter experience depended on multiple parties staying coordinated. Qingke had to secure homes, prepare them, collect rent, pay property owners, and provide support. If the company could not meet an owner payment, the renter could still lose access to a home even after paying Qingke. A centralized brand could make the experience simpler when the service worked, but it also became the point of failure when obligations broke apart.

The public record shows a long corporate unwind rather than one clean shutdown announcement. Q&K’s annual report disclosed that in January 2022 its subsidiary, Shanghai Qingke Public Rental Housing Leasing Management, filed a voluntary bankruptcy petition. A Shanghai court accepted the case, and the appointed administrator later called creditors’ meetings. The court’s public notice records the proceeding directly. The bankruptcy administrator’s notice confirms that this was a formal liquidation process.

The SEC filing also records that the group transferred interests in some Chinese operating entities to a related party in 2021, then disclosed additional subsidiary bankruptcy proceedings through 2022. Those are corporate restructuring facts; the filings do not by themselves establish why each transfer occurred. By December 2023, the court had terminated the main Qingke Public Rental liquidation proceeding. This timeline is why the story needs to name the entity involved rather than describe every company connected to the Qingke brand as having closed on the same date. The appointed administrator’s case summary records that termination.

Keep the operator separate from the holding company

Qingke is a useful case in corporate precision. Q&K transferred interests in some Chinese operating entities to a related party in 2021 and disclosed the subsequent bankruptcy of Qingke Public Rental. Its 2022 annual report also described other subsidiaries entering similar proceedings. The parent changed its name to FLJ Group in September 2022. Those events show that the apartment operation was breaking apart, but they do not mean the listed parent itself had been liquidated. The legal entities, rental brand, and listed holding company had different outcomes.

This distinction does not reduce the seriousness for tenants and property owners. A renter deals with the company that controls a particular apartment and contract, not the name at the top of a corporate chart. But a failure story should not blur a subsidiary bankruptcy into a claim that the holding company ceased to exist. The factual conclusion is that major mainland operating entities entered liquidation and the apartment business was materially dismantled; the parent continued under a new name.

What Domi should carry forward

For Domi, Qingke’s record makes the contract counterparty important. A listing should identify which legal entity or owner is offering the room, which party will collect rent, and which party handles maintenance and deposits. If ownership or management changes, the renter should be shown exactly what moved and what did not.

The product lesson is also about evidence. A platform can show that a room is furnished or that a landlord has used a service before, but those claims should be tied to the property and the current operator. Matching and discovery do not replace the need to verify who has authority over the lease. That interpretation follows from Qingke’s reported disputes: when the company-level story and the apartment-level responsibility diverge, the renter needs the apartment-level answer.

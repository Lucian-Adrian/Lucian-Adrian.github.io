RadPad survived a lawsuit, a sale and a relaunch, then shut down again

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 4 min read
RadPad · 2021Copy link

RadPad began with a simple renter problem: apartment listings were scattered, hard to compare and often difficult to see before a viewing. The Los Angeles startup built a photo-led mobile rental search app, then added payment tools, digital applications and lease signing. It was trying to turn apartment search into a transaction. By 2016, however, its payment service had stopped during legal and financial trouble. LandlordStation acquired RadPad’s assets in 2017 and relaunched the rent payment service. Priority Technology bought the operating assets in 2018. In December 2020, the RadPad team told users that property search, tenant screening and payments would all end after 15 January 2021.

A search app with a wider ambition

Founded in 2013, RadPad used photographs and maps to help renters find apartments nearby. The app expanded into rent payments that worked even when the landlord was not a RadPad customer. It later offered tenant applications, digital lease signing, screening and property tools. In 2016, the company described an end-to-end marketplace: renters could discover a home, apply, sign a lease and pay rent through one service. Its product announcement shows how far beyond simple listings it wanted to go.

There was a sound product intuition here. Renting involves a sequence of separate actions, and putting search, payments and documents in one place could reduce effort for both sides. The difficulty was that each added step brought new counterparties, compliance work and failure points. RadPad depended on listing supply, payment processors, landlords and tenants all continuing to trust the same system.

RadPad also tried to serve small and medium landlords, a group that did not always have its own software for applications, leases and rent collection. That gave the platform a sharper purpose than another apartment catalog. Yet the same end-to-end ambition multiplied dependencies: third-party inventory brought legal risk, while rent payments required funds to arrive reliably. The failures in those systems were related, but each needed its own safeguards.

Data access became a legal and financial risk

RadPad relied heavily on third-party listings, including data gathered from Craigslist. Craigslist sued, alleging its listings had been collected without permission. RadPad’s CEO denied wrongdoing in interviews, while reporting described significant legal expenses and layoffs. In 2017, a federal court entered a judgment of about $60.5 million in Craigslist’s favor. That judgment was a court outcome, but the available reporting does not show that RadPad paid the full amount.

TechCrunch’s 2017 account said the legal fees had put the company close to shutting down. It also reported that RadPad had raised more than $12 million and had about half a million active search users before the dispute, according to its CEO. The same article said the payment feature had paused and LandlordStation intended to restore it. RadPad had traction and a broad product vision, but depending on another company’s listings made its core marketplace vulnerable to a legal challenge that it could not control.

An acquisition restarted some services, not the original company

LandlordStation bought RadPad’s assets in late 2016 and publicly announced the transaction in January 2017. It kept the RadPad brand and relaunched rent payments in March. That was a meaningful restart for users, but it was an acquisition of assets, not proof that the original independent business had recovered. In July 2018, Priority Technology’s investor filing disclosed that it acquired substantially all operating assets of RadPad and LandlordStation. Priority planned to run them together under a real estate technology subsidiary.

The chronology matters because RadPad’s story was not one clean shutdown. The standalone startup nearly failed, a new owner continued the brand and brought back payments, and a second acquirer incorporated the assets into a larger platform. By late 2020, however, the service provider notified customers that it would stop accepting rent payments and screening orders, and disable property searches after 15 January 2021. The shutdown notice was reproduced by Frequent Miler. The listing business and payments service had both ended by then.

What Domi can take from this

Domi should know where every listing comes from and under what terms it can be used. Imported observations should retain their source, date and uncertainty. If a platform depends on third-party inventory, an access dispute can damage its core value at once. Permissioned feeds, owner or agent participation, and renter-submitted feedback can each improve the product, but Domi needs to be clear about which source supports which claim.

RadPad also shows why payment functionality changes the obligations of a housing app. Search can inform a renter. Payments create expectations about delivery, timing, records and support. Domi can first test whether it helps renters and operators reach real inquiries and viewings. It can add payment services only when the legal, operational and support responsibilities are understood. An all-in-one experience is useful when the underlying relationships are stable. The screen alone cannot make them stable.

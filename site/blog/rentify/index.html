Rentify promised landlords guaranteed rent, then its liabilities stayed behind

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 4 min read
Rentify · 2022Copy link

Rentify was founded in London in 2011 with a confident pitch: technology could make letting easier and cheaper for landlords. It offered advertising, tenant management, rent collection and other services through an online platform. Over time, its model included guaranteeing rent to landlords, creating a stronger promise than a typical listing or management service. In early 2022 the operating business went into administration. Estate agency Dexters bought its trade and assets, but not its contractual liabilities. Landlords were left trying to recover rent they said had not been passed on.

A lower-cost agent with a strong promise

Rentify presented itself as a virtual lettings agent. The company said landlords could cut overheads, manage a tenancy online and avoid some of the friction of a traditional agency. By 2022 it claimed at one point to have served 175,000 landlords. The Negotiator reported that Rentify charged a 15 percent management fee, higher than the average cited in the article, while arguing that its technology could still reduce the landlord’s overall costs.

The rent guarantee made the service easier to understand and more appealing to an owner who wanted predictable income. It also moved risk onto Rentify. A company that promises to pay an owner on time must still pay even when a tenant pays late, a home is vacant, or the market changes. Rentify therefore needed reliable cash reserves and strong control of rent flows, not just a good portal and a large customer base.

It also had to balance the guarantee against a management fee that its own industry coverage described as higher than average. The fee could still be attractive if digital operations reduced enough other costs. But vacancies and late rent turned each promise into a cash need, even when service revenue arrived later. The larger the portfolio became, the more important reliable payment timing was.

The economics depended on an obligation the software could not remove

Rentify’s marketing treated cost reduction as the way to make a generous guarantee work. But its obligations did not shrink automatically when occupancy, collection or cash flow changed. By March 2022, an interested buyer quoted by Property Industry Eye said Rentify had made cumulative losses exceeding £15 million across its life and pointed to flaws in the business model. The same report says Rentify had raised more than £10 million, including crowdfunding. Those figures came from the reporting about a prospective asset buyer, not audited financial analysis in the article, so they should be treated as reported context.

In February 2022, the online service showed few signs of active operation. The Negotiator reported that the site had no rental listings, its public information was outdated, directors had resigned, and calls to the company were not answered. The company entered administration on 27 January 2022, according to Companies House. Administrative filings show that the insolvency process later ended in September 2023, followed by a company voluntary arrangement that ended in July 2024. The legal entity’s later status is different from the failure of its former operating business.

The portfolio changed hands, but the promises did not

Dexters acquired Rentify’s trade and assets, including its landlord portfolio. It did not take on Rentify’s contractual liabilities. In The Negotiator’s account of the sale, Dexters said it could not offer Rentify’s rent guarantee because the model was not commercially viable. A landlord interviewed for the story said payments had started arriving late in June 2021 and that he learned of the handover months later. The report also describes Rentify ceasing communication with Dexters.

This is the central failure, beyond the company’s balance sheet. The assets that made a portfolio attractive to a buyer could move, while the promises made by the old company remained with the old company. A new manager could take over properties without automatically becoming responsible for unpaid rent or guarantees. A landlord and tenant might still be living under agreements involving an intermediary that no longer responded.

What Domi can take from this

Domi should keep a clear line between helping renters find and assess homes and taking responsibility for the property or its payments. If an owner or agency guarantees rent, Domi should identify that party and state the terms rather than imply that the platform itself guarantees an outcome. It can show who owns the property, who manages the listing, who receives rent and where a renter should go when the manager changes.

The business lesson is to test paid operator services before assuming that a larger promise creates a better product. A bounded service that qualifies inquiries or organizes viewings can be evaluated through operator time saved and completed viewings. A rent guarantee would require underwriting, reserves, contracts and recovery processes that go well beyond a marketplace. Rentify shows that a visible digital workflow cannot remove the underlying financial risk. Domi can learn from the operational promise without inheriting it.

REZI guaranteed apartment occupancy with capital it could not keep replacing

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 5 min read
REZI · 2023Copy link

REZI wanted to remove the friction from long-term apartment rentals. It used software to estimate rents and vacancy timelines, leased vacant units from property owners, guaranteed a set payment, and then found renters through an online process. Tenants could search, tour, apply and lease without broker fees, while owners were promised less vacancy and a simpler way to fill units. The model aimed to make rental operations faster, but it also put REZI between a landlord’s fixed income and a renter’s monthly payment. By November 2023, its credit lines had run dry. The Real Deal reported the shutdown on 1 April 2024.

A marketplace that took on the landlord’s risk

REZI was founded in 2016 and joined Y Combinator’s Winter 2017 batch. The company described itself as an all-digital marketplace for long-term apartments, using predictive technology to estimate rents, occupancy and credit losses. Its landlord promise was more than a listing: REZI could pay owners rent upfront or guarantee a monthly amount, then earn from the spread if it rented units for more. Renters got a digital process with contactless tours and automated applications. Y Combinator’s company profile describes this version of the product.

The approach addressed a real problem for large property owners. Vacant units can mean lost rent and staff time, while applications and tours are repeated tasks. REZI packaged a faster leasing process with a revenue guarantee. It also used technology to make decisions at scale and said it could reduce leasing costs. The service was ambitious because it combined rental software with a balance-sheet commitment.

Debt helped fuel expansion, but also raised the stakes

REZI expanded into multiple US markets and announced major financing commitments. In November 2022, the company announced $100 million in debt financing from Stratos Credit to support expansion. Three months later, Forbes reported another $100 million financing arrangement with SR Alternative Credit. That second structure was connected to lease-backed bonds and an investment model REZI was developing. These were debt and credit facilities, not two simple equity rounds that could be spent without repayment obligations.

Capital gave REZI a way to take on more units and enter new markets. But every new lease also increased the company’s fixed commitments. It had to pay owners even when apartments sat empty, while it also needed renters to move in and pay. The spread between guaranteed landlord rent and tenant rent needed to cover vacancies, operations and financing. The model therefore relied on accurate underwriting and continuing access to capital. Software could help estimate demand, but it could not make rent obligations disappear.

The funding failure became an operating failure

The Real Deal’s original shutdown report said REZI had run out of money and was shutting down. A later report about a landlord foreclosure dispute described REZI’s credit lines running dry in November 2023. That later account places the funding crisis in late 2023, even though The Real Deal’s shutdown report appeared in April 2024. The exact legal closure date is less clear than the business’s failure to keep operating.

The same later report describes allegations by Leagem Properties, a landlord that worked with REZI. Leagem’s attorney claimed that tenant rent was deposited into accounts tied to REZI’s lenders and used to pay down credit lines rather than transferred into a trust for the landlord. Those are allegations in litigation, not a court finding. They illustrate how difficult it became to follow rent when the intermediary’s financing system failed. The story also shows why a rental platform’s payment flows and landlord agreements need to remain legible if its lender or operating status changes.

There was no single product defect that explains REZI’s end. Its model depended on the property-level margin being strong enough to support long-term promises, on continued access to credit, and on rents being properly routed. Its debt facilities let it expand, but also made the company vulnerable when credit stopped flowing. The shutdown is best understood as a failure of financing and operational risk control around a valuable leasing product, rather than evidence that digital rental search itself was unwanted.

What Domi can take from this

Domi can remain useful to renters and property operators without leasing apartments or guaranteeing rent. REZI shows the difference between a marketplace that helps people find and assess homes and an operator that promises owners a fixed return. The latter requires underwriting, liquidity, contracts and reliable handling of payments. Those capabilities should not be implied by a search app or an inquiry assistant.

For a Domi pilot, the safer test is whether a renter passport and inquiry workflow improve response time, fit and completed viewings for one operator. The product can also show who owns a home, who manages it and who receives rent. If Domi ever considers holding or routing payments, it should first map the funds, reconciliation and failure process with the responsible providers. A better interface can reduce friction. It cannot replace the cash reserves and accountability needed to stand behind a rent promise.

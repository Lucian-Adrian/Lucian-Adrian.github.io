Upad closed twice, and the name kept moving

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 4 min read
Upad · 2025Copy link

Upad was built around a practical question: could a landlord complete the work of letting a home online, without paying for every step of a traditional high-street agency? Founded by landlord James Davis in 2008, the company became one of the UK’s best-known online letting agencies. It eventually stopped taking new business in October 2019. That was not the final chapter for the brand. Howsy bought key assets from the liquidators in January 2020 and continued Upad as a separate service. After Howsy ran into financial difficulty and was sold to Dexters, Upad announced a final closure effective 27 June 2025.

The service made self-service lettings tangible

Upad’s model let landlords advertise a property, arrange tenant referencing, prepare tenancy paperwork and choose optional services through an online platform. The basic offer was a lower-cost tenant-finding package, with more services available to add. In 2019, The Negotiator reported that Upad claimed 12,500 landlords had used it and that it had listed 30,000 properties. The same report said around 450 landlords might be affected by the closure notice.

The value proposition was easy to understand: help landlords handle routine tasks at a lower price and let them remain in control of decisions such as which tenant to accept. For customers comfortable managing the process themselves, the service could be cheaper and more flexible than a full-service agency. Upad’s popularity also showed that online lettings did not need to replace every local agent to matter. It could meet a narrower need for landlords willing to do some of the work themselves.

The first closure was both personal and commercial

In October 2019, Upad stopped accepting new landlords. Davis told Property Industry Eye that a serious heart condition and recovery from major surgery had changed his ability and desire to keep leading the company. He also said that he and other stakeholders could not agree on a path forward. He had hoped someone else might continue the business, but there was no agreed rescue or buyer before it closed.

The closure affected more than the founder. Sixteen employees lost their jobs. Upad stopped collecting rent by direct debit and told landlords and tenants in managed tenancies to contact one another directly about future arrangements. The company said it had ring-fenced rents, deposits and salaries, but some landlords who had prepaid for tenant-finding services before October 1 and had not yet found tenants could not recover those fees. The company’s records show that Upad Limited entered creditors’ voluntary liquidation on 30 October 2019.

Davis explicitly rejected speculation that regulation or the tenant fee ban had forced the closure. That is important because it keeps the explanation anchored in what he said: a health crisis, loss of personal drive and failure to agree on a continuation plan. It does not mean the business had no commercial pressure. Rather, the public reporting does not support replacing the founder’s account with a confident claim that a particular law or pricing change caused the end.

An asset sale turned an ending into a relaunch

In January 2020, Howsy bought Upad’s name, contracts and operating platform from the liquidators. The asset purchase report said Howsy intended to preserve the brand and build on its reputation. That distinction matters: the original Upad Limited had gone into liquidation, while a successor operator used the Upad brand and platform.

That revival later became vulnerable to Howsy’s own finances. The agency was sold to Dexters in 2022 as a distressed business. By late 2023, Dexters closed Howsy’s call centre and made the Howsy, Urban and Upad teams redundant. In April 2025, a representative confirmed Upad would close for good on 27 June. The final closure report cited Howsy’s financial difficulties and noted the group’s last filed accounts showed negative equity of £8.4 million. The brand survived its founding company, but it could not remain insulated from the operator’s later problems.

What Domi can take from this

Upad’s story suggests that a renter-facing marketplace and a landlord-facing service need clear boundaries. Domi can make renter discovery free and useful, then test a specific paid operator service such as inquiry qualification or viewing coordination. It should measure whether operators will pay for saved time and qualified inquiries before building a broad property-management stack. Upad’s online workflow had a clear customer and a tangible task, but its service still depended on compliance, support, refunds and communication when ownership changed.

For Domi, that means documenting who manages each listing, who receives payments, and which responsibilities belong to a landlord, an agent or the platform. If an operator or product changes hands, renters should still know where their questions go. This is a product and operations question that can be tested in a small local pilot. It is not evidence that Domi has already found a durable commercial model.

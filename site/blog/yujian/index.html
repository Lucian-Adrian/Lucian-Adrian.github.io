Yujian’s liquidity crisis started before landlords stopped being paid

Domi editorial11 October 2026 · 5 min read
Yujian Apartment · 2018Copy link

Yujian, the apartment brand run by Shanghai’s Xiaoyu Technology, was built around a common urban need: a furnished room with a known price and one company responsible for day-to-day service. By 2018, the operator said it managed more than 20,000 rooms and dozens of locations. Then it stopped paying landlords. A financing round that had been expected to support expansion fell through, a proposed transfer of thousands of leases became contentious, and residents found themselves between Yujian and the property owners. The company’s operational collapse was followed by a partial restructuring of homes and leases, not a clean rescue of the original business.

A large rental operator built in Shanghai

Yujian began in 2014 and grew into a large long-term rental operator in eastern China. It took apartments from property owners, furnished them, and let rooms to individual tenants. Caixin reported in October 2018 that the business had stopped paying landlords, faced deposit and salary complaints, and had pledged its equity as collateral for bank loans. The company’s owner-facing notice said it had severe cash shortages and no money available for rent payments. Caixin’s contemporaneous report describes the immediate crisis.

The offer had a reasonable premise. Moving into a new city can require a renter to find furniture, utilities, a roommate, and a landlord at once. A managed apartment could bundle those parts and give the owner a predictable channel for occupancy. Yujian also had operational experience and scale: Economic Observer reported 43 locations and more than 20,000 opened rooms. A larger network could help it negotiate with suppliers and reuse renovation standards across homes.

Growth depended on financing that did not arrive

The company had been seeking capital to continue expanding. Its co-founder told Economic Observer that an investment of 150 million yuan had been agreed in June, then fell through after rental operators came under scrutiny following the Hangzhou Dingjia collapse and other industry disputes. Reporting said the planned growth target attached to that investment would have expanded the portfolio to 200,000 rooms within three years. Those numbers came from the founder’s account and reporting, not from a completed financing or an achieved expansion.

That distinction matters because Yujian’s existing homes still carried expenses. A long-term rental operator has to meet rent obligations even when units are vacant, and renovations and maintenance require cash before the next tenant moves in. Economic Observer reported that the company’s cash strain began earlier in 2018, with overdue deposit returns and pressure to find new capital. It also reported that Yujian tried to sell around 3,000 leases to Qingke as a way to raise liquidity. A lease transfer might have provided cash and reduced some operating burden, but it depended on a buyer, owner consent, and a reliable handoff to tenants.

The lease transfer set off a wider panic

By the end of September, Yujian had stopped paying landlords. Its leadership described the decision as part of a plan to sell assets and rebalance the company. Economic Observer reported that stopping payments was intended to make the portfolio easier to transfer, while the founder described a technical operating mistake and a deteriorating cash position. Caixin separately reported that the failed funding round and rent-loan obligations were part of the liquidity problem. These accounts do not establish that one single decision caused the collapse. They do show how a cash-conservation move could be interpreted by landlords as default and by tenants as a threat to their housing.

The intended Qingke transfer did not proceed as a simple acquisition. Later reporting said negotiations stalled and that another operator, Maijia, and Eggshell took over selected homes or assets. That was a partial transition: it did not automatically transfer every deposit, loan, employee obligation, or old contract. Jiemian’s follow-up reported that former residents who had already moved out still found themselves dealing with Yujian over deposits, while successor operators took only some of the rental assets. Economic Observer’s detailed account covers the cash crunch and proposed sale; Jiemian’s 2019 follow-up distinguishes the later asset handoff from resolving old claims.

A rescue of homes was not a rescue of every promise

Yujian had built something renters valued: furnished spaces and a single point of contact in a market where arranging a home can require many separate calls. It also tried to restructure rather than simply abandon every unit. But the operating structure relied on stable financing, reliable rent spreads, and a buyer willing to accept selected properties during a crisis. When those conditions failed, the company’s individual contracts no longer moved together.

The lesson is not that rental operators should never transfer inventory. A careful transfer can keep residents housed and allow owners to continue earning rent. The failure lies in treating an asset transfer as if it automatically transfers accountability. Residents need to know who now holds their deposit, who is owed loan payments, and which operator handles repairs. The successor and the original company may have different responsibilities.

What Domi should carry forward

Domi can make a handoff safer by attaching each offer to a specific home, agreement, and responsible party. If a listing moves to a new operator, the renter should see whether the deposit and lease moved too, or whether only the property management changed. A home should not remain marked as an active option merely because a database still contains its address.

This case also argues for clear states in the rental journey. A property can be under review, authorized by its owner, ready to rent, or already occupied. Those are different conditions. The interpretation from Yujian’s collapse is that truthful status and payment records protect renters and help operators avoid disputes when cash becomes tight.
